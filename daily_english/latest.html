💬 Oral Practice — CS Graduate Interview

Question
In distributed systems design, the CAP theorem states you can only guarantee two of three properties simultaneously. Can you explain what CAP stands for, walk through the fundamental trade-offs a system designer must make, and give a concrete example of how a real-world database chooses between consistency and availability?
Reference Answer

The CAP theorem, formulated by Eric Brewer in 2000 and formally proven by Gilbert and Lynch in 2002, states that a distributed data store can simultaneously guarantee only two of three properties: Consistency, Availability, and Partition Tolerance.

Consistency means every read receives the most recent write or an error — all nodes see the same data at the same time. Availability guarantees that every request receives a non-error response, though it may not reflect the latest write. Partition Tolerance means the system continues operating despite network partitions that prevent some nodes from communicating.

In practice, network partitions are inevitable in any real distributed system, so the choice effectively reduces to: during a partition, do you prioritize consistency or availability? This divides systems into two families. CP systems — such as HBase, ZooKeeper, and etcd — refuse to serve stale data; during a partition they reject requests rather than return potentially incorrect results. This is critical for financial ledgers or distributed locks. AP systems — such as Apache Cassandra and CouchDB — continue serving requests but may return stale or divergent data, relying on eventual consistency and conflict-resolution mechanisms afterwards. These suit use cases like social media timelines or shopping carts.

The PACELC theorem extends this by noting that even without partitions, there is always a latency-versus-consistency trade-off. Modern systems like Google Spanner use TrueTime (atomic clocks + GPS) to approach strong consistency at global scale. Understanding CAP enables engineers to match database architecture to application SLA requirements.

Theoretical Principle: Yield Curve Inversion and Recession Signals
Under normal conditions, the yield curve slopes upward: investors demand a higher interest rate to lend money for longer periods, compensating for greater uncertainty and inflation risk over time. A yield curve inversion occurs when short-term interest rates (e.g., the 2-year Treasury yield) rise above long-term rates (e.g., the 10-year Treasury yield), typically signaling that bond markets expect the central bank will be forced to cut rates in the future — often because the economy is expected to weaken. Empirically, an inverted 2s10s spread has preceded every U.S. recession since the 1950s, usually with a lag of 12–18 months, making it one of the most watched leading indicators. The mechanism links to the Expectations Hypothesis of the term structure: long rates represent the market's average expectation of future short rates, so when long rates fall below current short rates, the market is implicitly forecasting monetary easing in response to economic slowdown. However, the predictive reliability of this signal has been debated, as structural factors such as central bank balance sheet expansion (quantitative easing) can suppress long-term yields artificially.

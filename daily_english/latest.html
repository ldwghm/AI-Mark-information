Topic: Economics / Management  |  Concept: Moral Hazard

❓ Interview Question

Can you explain the concept of moral hazard and how it arises in economic or business relationships? Please illustrate your answer with a concrete real-world example and discuss how institutions or policymakers attempt to mitigate it.

📝 Reference Answer

Moral hazard refers to the tendency of an individual or organization to take on more risk when they are insulated from its consequences — typically because another party bears the cost of that risk. It is a pervasive problem in economics that emerges whenever one party's behavior affects outcomes that are partially borne by others.

A classic example is the insurance industry. Once a homeowner purchases comprehensive fire insurance, they may become less diligent about fire-safety practices — leaving the stove on, skipping smoke-detector checks — because the financial downside of a fire is largely transferred to the insurer. The insurer cannot perfectly observe the homeowner's behavior, so the information asymmetry enables this risky conduct.

A second prominent example is the 2008 financial crisis. Large financial institutions took on excessive leverage partly because they assumed governments would bail them out — the "too big to fail" phenomenon. When expected losses are socialized but gains are privatized, the incentive to manage risk prudently is substantially weakened.

Common mitigation strategies include: co-payments and deductibles (making the risk-taker retain a portion of losses), performance-based contracts (aligning agent incentives with principal outcomes), regulatory capital requirements (limiting leverage), and enhanced monitoring and disclosure obligations. The fundamental goal is always to realign the interests of the party taking risks with those bearing the consequences.

💡 Theoretical Principle: Scaling Laws in Deep Learning

Scaling Laws (Kaplan et al., 2020) describe the empirical relationship between model performance and three key resources: the number of parameters, the volume of training data, and the amount of compute used. The central finding is that performance on language tasks improves predictably and smoothly as these quantities increase — following a power-law relationship. This means doubling the compute budget yields a consistent, quantifiable improvement in loss, allowing researchers to forecast the capabilities of future models before training them. Importantly, scaling laws also reveal optimal compute allocation: for a fixed budget, there is a precise ratio of model size to data tokens that minimizes loss (the "Chinchilla" insight). The implication is that the AI industry's race to build ever-larger models is not arbitrary — it is grounded in a mathematical framework suggesting that intelligence itself may be an emergent property of scale.

💡 Theoretical Principle: Yield Curve Dynamics & Expectations Theory

The Pure Expectations Theory of the yield curve holds that long-term interest rates are simply geometric averages of expected future short-term rates. Under this framework, the shape of the yield curve encodes the market's collective forecast for monetary policy: an inverted curve (short rates above long rates) implies the market expects the central bank to cut rates in the future, typically because economic weakness is anticipated. A "bull steepener" — where the curve steepens because short rates fall faster than long rates — occurs when investors price in imminent rate cuts while long-end yields remain anchored by stable long-run inflation expectations. Crucially, the Expectations Theory is supplemented by the Liquidity Preference Theory, which adds a term premium: investors demand extra compensation for locking up capital longer, which is why the curve normally slopes upward even when no rate changes are expected. Monitoring yield curve shape remains one of the most reliable leading indicators of economic turning points available to investors.

Question
Can you explain the principal-agent problem and discuss how it manifests in corporate governance? What mechanisms do companies commonly use to mitigate this issue, and what are the trade-offs involved?
Reference Answer

The principal-agent problem arises when one party — the agent — is hired to act on behalf of another — the principal — but their interests diverge. This misalignment is compounded by information asymmetry: the agent typically knows more about their own effort and intentions than the principal can observe. The result is suboptimal outcomes for the principal, ranging from shirking and excessive risk-avoidance to outright self-dealing.

In corporate governance, the classic manifestation is the relationship between shareholders and professional managers. Shareholders want to maximize long-run firm value, while executives may prioritize empire-building, job security, or short-term earnings manipulation. This separation of ownership and control was famously documented by Berle and Means in 1932 and remains central to modern finance.

Several mechanisms attempt to realign incentives. Performance-based compensation — stock options, restricted share units, or bonuses tied to long-term metrics — makes the agent's payoff mirror the principal's objective. Independent boards and audit committees provide external monitoring, while institutional investors can exercise activist pressure. Debt financing disciplines managers through the threat of bankruptcy, reducing free cash flow available for wasteful spending. Finally, the market for corporate control — hostile takeovers — punishes poor governance externally.

Each solution carries costs, however. Stock options may incentivize excessive short-term risk-taking or earnings manipulation. Monitoring is expensive and susceptible to board capture. The optimal governance structure depends on firm size, ownership concentration, and industry dynamics — meaning there is no one-size-fits-all solution to the agency problem.

Theoretical Principle
Jevons Paradox & Technology Diffusion
The Jevons Paradox, originally observed by economist William Stanley Jevons in 19th-century coal markets, states that technological improvements in resource efficiency tend to increase total resource consumption rather than reduce it — because lower costs expand demand far more than efficiency saves. Applied to AI, each order-of-magnitude reduction in inference cost historically triggers several orders of magnitude more inference demand, as new use cases become economically viable. The MoE efficiency breakthrough exemplifies this dynamic: while individual model runs become cheaper, the total compute consumed by the AI industry continues growing exponentially. This has direct implications for energy infrastructure planning, semiconductor supply chains, and climate policy. Understanding Jevons' Paradox helps economists and policymakers recognize that efficiency gains alone are insufficient to reduce total resource use without complementary demand-side interventions.

Theoretical Principle
Bond Duration & the Fisher Effect
Bond duration measures a fixed-income security's price sensitivity to changes in interest rates — specifically, it approximates the percentage price change for a 1% shift in yield. A 10-year Treasury bond with a modified duration of approximately 8.5 years will lose roughly 8.5% of its market value if yields rise by 1 percentage point. This explains why the Fed's hawkish signal caused immediate and severe mark-to-market losses across long-duration bond portfolios. Underlying the rate environment is the Fisher Effect, proposed by economist Irving Fisher, which holds that nominal interest rates equal the sum of real interest rates and expected inflation. When markets revise their inflation expectations upward — as appears to be the case now — nominal yields must rise to maintain real returns for investors. The interaction of duration risk and the Fisher Effect creates the mechanism through which monetary policy surprises transmit to asset prices across the entire economy, affecting mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs, and equity discount rates simultaneously.
